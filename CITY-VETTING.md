# Why these 109 cities?

At face value the list is peculiar. Bisbee, Arizona (pop. ~5k metro area)
is in; Nashville is not. Marquette, Michigan is in; Memphis is not. New
Mexico, a state with famously good weather, has zero entries. This document
explains the thinking, because the list is the most opinionated thing in
the project and the opinions are the point.

The list is generated by a separate, private vetting project and imported
here as `data/cities.json`. What follows describes its method. (Forks
don't need any of this: edit `cities.json` directly and the scorer will
happily rank whatever you give it.)

## What the list is for

Sunchaser is a discovery instrument, not a travel guide. Its job is to
surface a place-time pair: somewhere you might not have considered, having
its best week right now. That only works if every city on the list is
somewhere a traveler could show up **sight-unseen** and comfortably live
for a month. The moment one entry needs an asterisk ("great weather,
but..."), the crown stops meaning "go here" and the product breaks.

So the bar is not "is this a great city?" It is "would you trust this city
without researching it further?" Famous destinations that miss the bar
are excluded without regret, on the theory that nobody needs a weather
model to discover Nashville. If you go to Nashville, you go on purpose,
knowing the trade-offs. The list is for the places you *wouldn't* have
thought of.

## Stage 1: hard gates (fail any one, you're out)

Candidates (about 130 US cities with plausible nomad appeal) first pass
through gates. A gate failure is recorded with its reason; nothing is
silently dropped.

- **Services floor.** Metro/CBSA population ≥ 75k, OR a seeded "genuine
  hub" services score for small towns that punch above their weight
  (this is how Bisbee and Sedona survive while other small towns don't).
- **Vehicle-service reach.** A manufacturer service center for the
  operator's EV within a 120-minute drive. This is the gate that excludes
  otherwise-lovely remote towns (Yuma, Bend, Taos, Durango). It is a
  proxy for a broader truth: a month-long stay depends on the modern
  service web, not just scenery. It's the same species of proxy as "is
  there an Apple Store within reach": premium service networks site
  themselves on exactly the sophistication a visitor quietly depends on.
  The calibration matters, though: an Apple-Store gate would demand a
  major metro and would have vetoed Duluth and Marquette, while EV
  service reaches into smaller capable towns. The proxy was chosen to
  find sophistication without requiring scale.
- **Safety, two layers.** An explicit hard-veto list for extreme cases,
  plus a *relative* danger tier: a violent-weighted composite of
  violent/property/auto-theft rates per 100k, excluding roughly the worst
  15% of the candidate field (with a sanity floor so the tier can't
  over-prune). This is what excludes Nashville, New Orleans, Memphis, and
  Santa Fe: all wonderful places to visit on purpose, all statistically
  poor places to park a stranger for a month. A standalone auto-theft
  gate existed until 2026-08 and was removed deliberately: car theft
  alone wasn't the real threat model, and the composite already counts
  it. Gates should each be a rule the operator would defend out loud.
- **Season viability.** A city must have at least one season where its
  climate is genuinely good; it carries `season_tags` for the seasons it
  plausibly competes in (this drives Sunchaser's active/dormant fetch
  tiers).

Crime data is FBI-derived, point-in-time, and periodically refreshed;
a rejection is a snapshot, not a life sentence. If a city's numbers
improve, the gates readmit it on the next rebuild with no human mercy
required.

## Stage 2: scored factors (for the survivors)

Gate survivors are scored on weighted factors, roughly: climate comfort
(20), cost (16), safety (12), disaster risk (12), culture/scene (12),
sunshine (10), tax/domicile flexibility (8), services (5), logistics (5).
Two design details worth stealing:

- **Disaster risk is occupancy-seasonal.** A Gulf Coast city used in
  winter is not penalized for hurricane season, because you wouldn't be
  there. Each hazard has a monthly activity curve, and only the months
  you'd occupy count. (The annual insurance reality stays priced into
  cost.)
- **Curation must not be load-bearing.** The top ~30 by score form a
  "curated" set (starred in Sunchaser's table; the AI verdict may prefer
  them only to break effective ties). The other ~79 gate-passers ship
  too. The build asserts that dropping the curated list entirely would
  still leave a sane pool, so taste never silently becomes a gate.

## Why the map has holes

- **New Mexico (zero cities):** all five candidates failed gates: Santa
  Fe, Las Cruces, and Albuquerque on crime, Taos and Silver City on
  service reach. Audited 2026-08: NM's two climate zones are still
  covered by analogs (Flagstaff and the Colorado front range for the
  high plateau, El Paso for the Chihuahuan desert), so the *weather* is
  never invisible, only the state's name.
- **Ohio Valley and central Plains:** no candidates, accepted
  structural gap. That climate is dominated by in-pool neighbors on
  essentially every day; a week Louisville would win is a week
  Chattanooga wins bigger.
- **Hawaii and Alaska:** out of scope; this is a drive-there product.

## The honest caveats

The list encodes one operator's risk tolerance and taste, measured with
public data and frozen into gates. City-level crime rates are blunt
(crime is hyper-local; a citywide number can't see a safe neighborhood).
Weather data can't see building stock (a cool-summer city may lack air
conditioning on its rare hot day). The gates are recalibrated
occasionally, always by changing a rule, never by smuggling in an
exception: the day one famous city gets carried in on vibes, the crown
stops meaning anything for all 109.
